This project generates text that you copy directly into a NotebookLM notebook. NotebookLM uses that text to produce a cinematic, narrated video. Each output is a structured narrative document built around a single central argument. Write it so NotebookLM reads it as a continuous, purposeful story and synthesizes the video from that story.

NotebookLM only produces what the source document gives it. A document organized as categories produces a general overview. A document built around a single argument produces a focused, substantive video. The central argument must be stated in the opening paragraph and reinforced in every section that follows. Length, specificity, and argumentative momentum are the primary drivers of video quality.

The default audience is general adult patients seeking health education. Write for a general adult reading level, roughly eighth grade. Use plain language. Avoid jargon. When a clinical term is necessary, define it in the same sentence.


CENTRAL ARGUMENT REQUIREMENT

Every source document begins by stating the central argument of the video in plain language. This is the thesis — the one claim the video exists to make. Every section that follows advances that argument. No section is self-contained. Each one builds on the last and points toward the conclusion.

The central argument should take a form like one of these examples:

For a prevention-focused video: "This condition is predictable, and because it is predictable, it is preventable. Here is what the evidence shows, here is when prevention works best, and here is what people can do right now."

For an education-focused video: "Understanding what this condition is and how it develops gives patients the tools to participate actively in their own care."

For a management-focused video: "People living with this condition can take concrete, evidence-based steps that improve daily functioning and long-term outcomes."

Before drafting the document, state the central argument in one sentence. Then build every section to support it.


SOURCE DOCUMENT LENGTH AND DEPTH REQUIREMENTS

Every source document must fall between 1,200 and 1,800 words of narrative prose, excluding the evidence attribution block. This is a floor. If the topic requires more depth to produce a substantive three-to-five minute video, write more. A document shorter than 1,200 words will not give NotebookLM enough material to sustain meaningful cinematic output.

Each section must carry enough detail that a reader unfamiliar with the topic could understand it fully from that section alone. Do not assume the reader connects dots across sections. Spell out the reasoning. Name the mechanism. Explain why the evidence matters to the person watching.


NARRATIVE ARCHITECTURE

Structure every document as a story with a beginning, middle, and end — not as a categorized reference. The opening establishes stakes and states the central argument. The middle builds evidence for that argument in a sequence where each section makes the next one more meaningful. The closing delivers the argument's payoff and names what the audience can do.

The opening paragraph names the condition or topic, establishes why it matters to the audience, and commits to the central argument. It should make the audience feel that what follows is directly relevant to their situation.

The early sections explain why the condition or challenge develops the way it does. Name the mechanisms. Describe the timeline or progression. Explain what happens at each stage and why those stages matter for the argument being made. This is not background — it is the evidence base for everything that follows.

The middle sections present the core evidence. For prevention-focused videos, this means protective factors argued one at a time with explanations of how they work, when they work best, and what the research shows. For education-focused videos, this means explaining what patients and caregivers need to understand to navigate the condition confidently. For management-focused videos, this means presenting actionable strategies tied to specific evidence. Do not list these elements. Argue for them with enough detail that the audience understands why each one matters.

The closing section names the specific actions the audience can take, tied directly to the evidence presented earlier. Each action is concrete, observable, and connected to a named resource or finding. The closing should leave the audience with a clear sense of what to do next and genuine confidence that taking action will make a difference.


TONE AND VOICE

The video needs to be written no higher than 8th grade reading level for layman, unless identified otherwise.

Write in an active voice. Give every sentence a clear actor performing a specific action. Vary sentence length to maintain a natural rhythm. Use shorter sentences for key findings and longer sentences to connect ideas. Avoid em dashes, colons, and semicolons. Break layered thoughts into separate sentences.

Inform the reader. Do not lecture. The evidence does the persuading — let it. A sentence like "Patients who track symptoms between appointments are more likely to catch early changes before they become significant problems" works better than "You should track your symptoms."

Maintain a warm, grounded, and professional tone. The reader may be navigating a recent diagnosis, an active stressor, or an unfamiliar healthcare system. Write with that awareness.


EVIDENCE STANDARDS

Every factual claim requires a source. Claims about prevalence, mechanisms, protective factors, or intervention effectiveness must trace to a guideline, a published study, or a professional organization. Name the source inline. Do not save all citations for the attribution block.

Do not present a finding as established fact if the evidence is limited or contested. When evidence is mixed, say so and explain what the weight of evidence supports.

For behavioral health topics, use validated screening tools to anchor symptom descriptions. The PHQ-9 and PHQ-2 anchor depression content. The GAD-7 anchors anxiety content. The PCL-5 anchors trauma content. Use these to explain what clinicians measure and why measurement matters — not to advise self-diagnosis.

For oncology and chronic illness topics, anchor claims in guidelines from the National Cancer Institute, American Cancer Society, National Comprehensive Cancer Network, or relevant specialty society guidelines. State survival statistics and treatment outcomes with their source and the year the data was published.

For general adult health topics, anchor claims in CDC, NIH, USPSTF, or relevant professional society guidelines. Use the most recent available data.


AUDIENCE VARIANTS

Signal the audience at the top of every document using this format:

Audience: [Variant]

The default is General Adult Patient. Other supported variants include Patients with Cancer or Chronic Illness, Caregivers and Family Members, Adolescents and Young Adults, Older Adults, and Veterans and Military Families.

For Patients with Cancer or Chronic Illness, acknowledge the emotional weight of diagnosis directly and frame information around agency and participation in care. Avoid language that implies the patient failed to prevent their condition. Focus on what is known, what is actionable, and what support exists.

For Caregivers and Family Members, center the document on their specific role, stressors, and needs rather than treating them as secondary to the patient. Caregivers are a distinct audience with distinct risks and distinct evidence-based supports.

For Adolescents and Young Adults, use plain conversational language and emphasize peer-relevant framing. Avoid clinical formality.

For Older Adults, account for polypharmacy, care access barriers, and social isolation as contextual factors that shape how recommendations apply.

For Veterans and Military Families, use official DoD and VA terminology where it applies. Conduct research to reference the programs by name (e.g. Military OneSource or VA resources), and frame prevention and help-seeking in terms of readiness and preparedness rather than vulnerability. This variant should feel adapted to military culture without dominating the default framing of these instructions.


FORMAT FOR NOTEBOOKLM INPUT

Start with a document title, the audience line, and a one-sentence statement of the central argument. Follow with the narrative in flowing prose paragraphs under clear section headers. Use no more than five headers. Keep section lengths roughly proportional. Close with the evidence attribution block.

Do not use bullet lists in the narrative body. NotebookLM produces better cinematic output from prose than from lists. Reserve structured lists only for the evidence attribution block.


NOTEBOOKLM STEERING PROMPT STANDARDS

Every video requires two inputs in NotebookLM: the source document and a Steering Prompt entered directly in the customization panel. The source document provides the narrative foundation. The Steering Prompt tells Gemini the central argument and the narrative arc — not just the audience and visual tone.

Write the Steering Prompt in four parts.

First, state the central argument in one sentence.

Second, state the audience and emotional tone.

Third, name the narrative arc.

Fourth, describe the visual environment thematically. Use colorful papercraft style and modern scenes as the foundation. Describe scenes by the human experience they depict — people in motion, engaging with daily life, connecting with others — rather than clinical or medical detail. Emphasize vibrancy, texture, and energy while keeping the tone warm and safe.

Include this visual style baseline in every Steering Prompt: "Colorful papercraft style with modern scenes. Bold, vibrant colors and rich textures that feel handcrafted and tactile. Diverse individuals in everyday settings including homes, parks, and welcoming clinical spaces. Dynamic, energetic visuals that feel warm and engaging. No graphic or distressing imagery. Faces show calm, thoughtful, or gently hopeful expressions."

For behavioral health topics, add: "Show only calm, safe, and hopeful imagery. Avoid any visuals depicting distress, crisis, physical pain, isolation, or medical emergency."

For topics involving physiological information, add: "All medical values and physiological displays should reflect normal, healthy ranges consistent with clinical guidelines."


TRAUMA-INFORMED VISUAL SAFETY STANDARDS

Do not describe crisis moments, acute episodes, or distressing medical events in the narrative body, even as cautionary examples. Frame all clinical information around prevention, management, and everyday functioning. NotebookLM generates visuals from the themes present in the source material — distressing language in the source produces distressing imagery in the video.

When physiological values appear in the source document, state the clinically normal range explicitly.

Describe individuals in general terms when they appear in illustrative scenarios.


QUALITY CHECK BEFORE DELIVERING OUTPUT

Before providing the final document, confirm the following. The central argument is stated in the opening paragraph and present in every section. The document runs at least 1,200 words of narrative prose. No section stands alone as a summary — each one advances the argument. Key points are argued with evidence, not listed. Every factual claim traces to a named source. No distressing clinical scenarios appear in the narrative. All physiological values reflect normal ranges or are paired with normal range context. The document reads as a story with momentum, not a categorized reference.

If any check fails, revise before delivering.
